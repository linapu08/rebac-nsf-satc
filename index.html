Modeling and Mitigating Reflective Backscatter Side‑Channel Attacks in Everyday Devices

Consumer electronics are built on the assumption that an intact enclosure protects the data moving inside it. Unshielded flex cables and PCB traces break that assumption: illuminated by a low‑power radio signal, they reflect back what a person is typing, clicking, and sensing, from meters away and through walls.

Research Vision

A hard shell around a soft core

This project establishes a scientific foundation for understanding and defending against reflective backscatter side‑channel attacks on everyday devices.

The consumer electronics industry invests heavily in hardening external perimeters. Wireless links are encrypted, external interconnects such as USB are shielded. Past the plastic and glass, though, implicit trust takes over: designers assume an intact housing implies secure internal buses.

In practice, modular components are linked by unshielded flex cables and PCB traces. I²C carries sensor and actuator data, UART services detachable inputs such as game controller rails, and SPI connects touchscreens and cameras. Teardowns of ordinary keyboards, mice, controllers, and smart‑home devices show these conductors sitting exposed, an internal attack surface that perimeter‑focused defenses routinely overlook.

Prior electromagnetic side channels were dismissed as impractical for good reason. They are passive, listening for faint unintentional emanations, which forces an adversary to within centimeters or behind a meter‑scale antenna. Weak signals against a high noise floor kept remote eavesdropping on internal buses inside the laboratory.

ReBac inverts that. Instead of listening, a compact device illuminates the target with a low‑power radio signal that passes through non‑metallic enclosures. Impedance changes driven by digital activity modulate the reflected waveform, and a receiver demodulates it to reconstruct internal data streams from several meters away, without line of sight and without any implant in the device.

Capability 01

Digital exfiltration

Decoding the actual bitstreams on serial links carried over unshielded flex cables and PCB traces, recovering raw keystrokes, controller inputs, and sensor traffic as plaintext.

Capability 02

Analog inference

Recognizing repeatable analog signatures produced by usage itself, such as the current pattern of a keyboard matrix scan or the PWM drive inside a mouse, even where no serial bus is exposed.

Why it matters

Billions of devices

Keyboards, mice, detachable peripherals, IoT gadgets, and DIY systems all share the same internal construction. The vulnerability is a property of how devices are built, not of any one product.

What the pilot study established

Measured in the PIs' study published at the IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy in May 2025, whose Meta Review recognized the work for both Identifies an Impactful Vulnerability and Establishes a New Research Direction.

Research Objectives

Three objectives

ReBac is a newly identified threat with broad implications, so it needs a systematic investigation of its physical principles, its practical impact, and its mitigations. The project pursues three integrated objectives.

01

Theoretical framework

Build a predictive model linking victim‑device parameters such as cable length, geometry, and protocol; environmental conditions such as distance and physical obstacles; and attacker configuration such as transmit power, frequency, and receiver design, to overall attack feasibility. The goal is vulnerability assessment across device families without exhaustive experimental trials.

02

End‑to‑end demonstrations

Determine what sensitive user data can actually be recovered from real commodity products: keyboards, mice, controllers, and IoT gadgets. Demonstrating both the digital and analog capabilities under realistic operating conditions is what turns an abstract leak into a quantified privacy and security harm.

03

Cost‑effective defenses

Turn adversarial insight into countermeasures a manufacturer would actually adopt: safe‑length and layout guidelines for internal interconnects, lightweight shielding strategies, and data‑randomization techniques. The intended output is actionable guidance and reproducible test reports for hardening the next generation of devices.

Findings, datasets, and defense guidelines will be posted here and released publicly as they are published.

Related Education & Outreach

A cross‑state pipeline in two EPSCoR states

Ongoing education and outreach by the PIs that connects directly to the themes of this project. These activities are supported separately and are not funded by this award.

Curriculum · UA and MSU

A shared IoT and IoT Security course at two universities

The two PIs jointly developed a course on IoT and IoT security and have offered it regularly at both institutions since 2022, as CS 648 at the University of Alabama and ECE 8763 at Mississippi State University. Building a single course across two campuses lets students in Alabama and Mississippi work from common material, and gives the PIs a direct route from research findings to the classroom.

K‑12 outreach · Alabama

MathCounts STEM Demo

PI Pu serves as Coordinator for MathCounts Tuscaloosa and leads an interactive STEM Demo program connecting College of Engineering faculty with middle‑school students through immersive research demonstrations. Battery‑free IoT has been part of the lineup since 2023. The program received the University's 2024 Excellence in Community Engagement Award. She also organizes IoT and edge computing demonstrations at UA E‑Day with the Women in CyberSecurity student organization she mentors.

K‑12 outreach · Mississippi

MSTI Summer Camp and CyberTraining

PI Luo delivers IoT lab tours through the Mississippi Summer Transportation Institutes, residential camps that serve up to 20 high‑school students each year, more than half of them from groups underrepresented in computing. Participants get hands‑on exposure to embedded hardware, robotics, and the anechoic chamber where the group's wireless measurements are made.
